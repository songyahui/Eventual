       A Monadic Approach to Temporal Specification}

\begin{abstract}
We present \emph{FutureCond}, a Haskell library that extends the classical
pre/post-condition contract model with \emph{future conditions}: regular
expressions over events that specify what the remainder of a program must
eventually do.
Future conditions are carried as a first-class field in the
\lstinline{Effectful} monad alongside pre- and post-conditions.
When operations are composed via monadic bind, future conditions are
propagated and updated automatically using a
Brzozowski-derivative-based subtraction operator, and preconditions
accumulate in the Hoare-rule style: the residual of the continuation's
precondition not yet discharged by the current computation's postcondition.
A future condition that normalises to $\univ$ (the universal language)
certifies that all temporal obligations have been discharged; a
precondition normalising to $\varnothing$ flags a violation.
The regular-expression language is extended with a complement operator
whose derivative commutes algebraically with negation---no automaton
construction is required.
We demonstrate the approach across three domains---memory management,
mutexes, and database transactions---showing
that resource-lifecycle and ordering properties invisible to
pre/post reasoning become statically visible as residual annotations.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{temporal specification, monadic effects, regular expressions,
          complement, Brzozowski derivatives, resource lifecycle, Haskell}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%% =========================================================================

Pre- and post-conditions are the workhorse of modular program specification.
A pre-condition constrains the state in which an operation may be called;
a post-condition describes what holds once it returns. Together they reason
about a single \emph{call boundary}. Yet many correctness properties span
multiple calls separated by unbounded intervening computation:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Every \lstinline{malloc(a)} must eventually be followed by
        \lstinline{free(a)}.
  \item Every \lstinline{open(f)} must eventually be followed by
        \lstinline{close(f)}.
  \item Every \lstinline{beginTx} must eventually reach
        \lstinline{commit} or \lstinline{rollback}.
  \item Every \lstinline{acquire(m)} must eventually be followed by
        \lstinline{release(m)}.
\end{itemize}

These are \emph{deferred obligations}---temporal constraints on what the
\emph{rest} of the program must do after an operation is invoked.
Pre/post reasoning cannot express them: a post-condition records what is
true \emph{now}, not what must become true \emph{later}.

We propose \emph{future conditions} as a lightweight, compositional
mechanism for specifying such obligations.
A future condition is a regular expression over events that the remaining
execution trace must match.
It is carried as a third annotation---alongside \lstinline{pre} and
\lstinline{post}---in an \lstinline{Effectful} monad.

Two new design elements distinguish FutureCond from prior work.
First, the regular-expression language is extended with a
\emph{complement} operator $\neg r$, letting us write the universal
language as $\univ\;(=\neg\varnothing)$ and globally-always assertions
as $\neg(\univ\cdot\neg\phi)$, all without building automata:
the derivative of $\neg r$ is $\neg(\partial_a r)$, so the whole
apparatus is purely algebraic.
Second, the \lstinline{pre} field is propagated in the
\emph{Hoare-rule} style: the precondition of a sequenced computation
is the precondition of the first step, plus the residual of the
continuation's precondition not discharged by the first step's
postcondition.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item We define the \lstinline{Effectful} monad (\S\ref{sec:effectful}),
        parameterised over a \lstinline{Composable} algebra, with
        \lstinline{pre}, \lstinline{post}, and \lstinline{future} fields,
        and verify that the monad laws hold for the
        $(\ret, \post, \fut)$ triple (\S\ref{sec:monad-laws}).
  \item We instantiate the algebra to extended regular expressions over
        events with complement, using Brzozowski derivatives to implement
        subtraction---the core of future-condition propagation
        (\S\ref{sec:re})---and show that LTL\textsubscript{f} operators
        embed directly as RE terms (\S\ref{sec:ltl}).
  \item We demonstrate the approach on three use cases
        (\S\ref{sec:examples}), showing that both resource leaks and
        precondition violations surface as non-trivial residual annotations.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Extended Regular Expressions over Events}
\label{sec:re}

Events carry a name and typed arguments.

\begin{lstlisting}
data Term  = Var String | Str String | Num Int
data Event = Atom String [Term]   -- concrete event, e.g. Atom "send" [Num 1]
           | Wildcard             -- pattern matching any single event
\end{lstlisting}

The specification language is an \emph{extended} regular expression type
that adds complement as a first-class constructor:

\[
  r \;::=\; \varnothing \mid \varepsilon \mid e \mid r_1 \cdot r_2
          \mid r_1 \vee r_2 \mid r_1 \wedge r_2 \mid r^* \mid \neg r
\]

Here $e$ is a \lstinline{Single Event} that uses \lstinline{Wildcard} as a
pattern for any event, $\wedge$ is intersection (derived:
$r_1\wedge r_2 \triangleq \neg(\neg r_1\vee\neg r_2)$), and
$\neg r$ is the complement with respect to $\Sigma^*$.

\begin{lstlisting}
data RE = Bot | Epsilon | Single Event
        | Seq RE RE | Or RE RE | And RE RE | Star RE | Not RE
\end{lstlisting}

Three derived forms are central:

\begin{align*}
  \anything &\;\triangleq\; \neg\varnothing
            &&\text{(the universal language } \Sigma^*\text{)} \\
  \finally{e} &\;\triangleq\; \anything \cdot e \cdot \anything
            &&\text{($e$ must occur eventually)} \\
  \mathtt{globally}(e) &\;\triangleq\; e^*
            &&\text{($e$ at every step)}
\end{align*}

\subsection{Algebraic Complement and Brzozowski Derivatives}
\label{sec:deriv}

The Brzozowski derivative~\cite{brzozowski1964} $\partial_a(r)$
computes the language of continuations after reading event $a$.
The key law for complement is:

\begin{equation}
  \partial_a(\neg r) \;=\; \neg\bigl(\partial_a(r)\bigr)
  \label{eq:deriv-not}
\end{equation}

The nullability function $\nu(r)$ ($= \mathtt{True}$ iff
$\varepsilon \in L(r)$) satisfies $\nu(\neg r) = \neg\,\nu(r)$.
Together, equations~\eqref{eq:deriv-not} and the De Morgan laws

\[
  \neg(r_1\vee r_2) = \neg r_1\wedge\neg r_2
  \qquad
  \neg(r_1\wedge r_2) = \neg r_1\vee\neg r_2
\]

allow complement to be propagated and simplified \emph{purely
algebraically}, without building a DFA.
The normaliser additionally applies $\neg\neg r = r$,
$\neg\varnothing = \univ$, and $\neg\univ = \varnothing$.

In Haskell:

\begin{lstlisting}
nullable :: RE -> Bool
nullable (Not r)     = not (nullable r)      -- nu(Not r) = not(nu(r))
nullable (Star _)    = True
nullable (Seq r1 r2) = nullable r1 && nullable r2
nullable (Or  r1 r2) = nullable r1 || nullable r2
nullable (And r1 r2) = nullable r1 && nullable r2
nullable Epsilon     = True
nullable _           = False

derivative :: Event -> RE -> RE
derivative e (Not r)      = Not (derivative e r)   -- d_a(Not r) = Not(d_a(r))
derivative e (Single p)   = if matchEvent e p then Epsilon else Bot
derivative e (Seq r1 r2)
    | nullable r1 = Or (Seq (derivative e r1) r2) (derivative e r2)
    | otherwise   = Seq (derivative e r1) r2
derivative e (Or  r1 r2)  = Or  (derivative e r1) (derivative e r2)
derivative e (And r1 r2)  = And (derivative e r1) (derivative e r2)
derivative e (Star r)     = Seq (derivative e r) (Star r)
derivative _ _            = Bot
\end{lstlisting}

The \lstinline{firstWith} function returns the set of events---drawn from
a supplied finite alphabet $\Sigma_0$---that can begin a word in $L(r)$,
driving the subtraction algorithm.
The complement case requires care.
Naively writing \lstinline{first (Not r) = first r} is \emph{unsound}:
$L(\neg r)$ can begin with events \emph{not} in $\mathit{first}(r)$,
so the na\"ive rule explores the wrong direction.
The correct characterisation is:

\begin{equation}
  e \in \mathit{first}(\neg r,\,\Sigma_0)
  \;\iff\;
  e \in \Sigma_0
  \;\wedge\;
  \lceil\partial_e(r)\rceil \neq \univ
  \label{eq:first-not}
\end{equation}

That is, $e$ can open a word in $L(\neg r)$ iff after consuming $e$,
the residual $\partial_e(r)$ does \emph{not} cover the whole future---there
exists some continuation that stays outside $L(r)$.
The alphabet $\Sigma_0$ is collected from an RE via \lstinline{atoms},
which recursively gathers all concrete (\lstinline{Atom}) events,
excluding \lstinline{Wildcard}.

\begin{lstlisting}
atoms :: RE -> [Event]
atoms (Single Wildcard) = []
atoms (Single e)        = [e]
atoms (Seq r1 r2)       = atoms r1 `union` atoms r2
atoms (Or  r1 r2)       = atoms r1 `union` atoms r2
atoms (And r1 r2)       = atoms r1 `union` atoms r2
atoms (Star r)          = atoms r
atoms (Not r)           = atoms r
atoms _                 = []

firstWith :: [Event] -> RE -> [Event]
firstWith _    Bot               = []
firstWith _    Epsilon           = []
firstWith _    (Single e)        = [e]
firstWith alph (Seq r1 r2)
    | nullable r1                = firstWith alph r1 `union` firstWith alph r2
    | otherwise                  = firstWith alph r1
firstWith alph (Or  r1 r2)      = firstWith alph r1 `union` firstWith alph r2
firstWith alph (And r1 r2)      = [e | e <- firstWith alph r1,
                                        e `elem` firstWith alph r2]
firstWith alph (Star r)         = firstWith alph r
firstWith alph (Not r)          = [e | e <- alph,
                                        not (isTotal (normalize (derivative e r)))]
  where isTotal (Not Bot) = True; isTotal _ = False
\end{lstlisting}

\noindent
The function \lstinline{first r = firstWith (atoms r) r} is a convenient
wrapper for standalone use; inside \lstinline{subtraction} both operands
contribute to the alphabet (see \S\ref{sec:re-instance}).

\paragraph{Illustrative cases.}
Table~\ref{tab:firstwith-not} gives representative evaluations of
\lstinline{firstWith} with \lstinline{Not}, ordered from simple to
non-trivial.
The first three rows confirm that the complement of a single pattern,
a sequence, or a union does not exclude any alphabet event---every event
can open some word in the complement.
The double-negation row shows that \lstinline{firstWith} correctly restores
the original first set: $\partial_b(\neg a) = \neg\varnothing = \Sigma^*$
is total, so $b$ is filtered out by the $\lceil\cdot\rceil \neq \univ$
check.
The last two rows are the most instructive.
In $\neg(a \cdot \Sigma^*)$ (words not starting with $a$), the derivative
$\partial_a(a \cdot \Sigma^*)$ normalises to $\Sigma^*$---consuming $a$
leaves every future possible---so $a$ is \emph{excluded}; only $b$ survives.
Similarly, $\neg(a\cdot\Sigma^* \vee b\cdot\Sigma^*)$ excludes both $a$
and $b$, leaving only $c$.
The De Morgan row checks that \lstinline{firstWith} handles complement
pushed inside a conjunction: $\neg(\neg a \wedge \neg b) = a \vee b$
(by De Morgan and double negation), and indeed only $a$ and $b$ appear.

\subsection{Embedding LTL\textsubscript{f}}
\label{sec:ltl}

Because complement is a first-class constructor, the standard
LTL\textsubscript{f} operators translate directly into RE---no
automaton construction is needed.
Table~\ref{tab:ltl} gives the full mapping.

\begin{table}[h]
\caption{LTL\textsubscript{f} to RE translation.
         $\Sigma^1 \triangleq \mathtt{Single\;Wildcard}$,
         $\univ \triangleq \neg\varnothing$.}
\label{tab:ltl}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
LTL formula & RE $\sem{\cdot}$ \\
\midrule
$\top$              & $\univ$ \\
$\bot$              & $\varnothing$ \\
$e$                 & $\{e\}$ \\
$\neg\phi$          & $\neg\sem{\phi}$ \\
$\phi \wedge \psi$  & $\sem{\phi} \cap \sem{\psi}$ \\
$\phi \vee \psi$    & $\sem{\phi} \cup \sem{\psi}$ \\
$X\,\phi$           & $\Sigma^1 \cdot \sem{\phi}$ \\
$F\,\phi$           & $\univ \cdot \sem{\phi}$ \\
$G\,\phi$           & $\neg(\univ \cdot \neg\sem{\phi})$ \\
$\phi\,U\,\psi$     & $\mathit{step}(\phi)^* \cdot \sem{\psi}$
                      \quad ($\phi$ propositional) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The complement operator does the heavy lifting.
$\neg\phi$ maps to $\neg\sem{\phi}$ in one step.
$G\,\phi \triangleq \neg F\neg\phi$ unfolds to
$\neg(\univ\cdot\neg\sem{\phi})$---``no suffix violates
$\phi$''---using two applications of $\neg$ and no auxiliary
construction.
$F\,\phi$ and $X\,\phi$ need only concatenation.

The $U$ case is the exception.
$\phi\,U\,\psi$ requires $\phi$ to hold at each individual step
before $\psi$, so the translation uses
$\mathit{step}(\phi)$---the length-1 RE for a single event
satisfying $\phi$.
This is well-defined when $\phi$ is propositional; for temporal
$\phi$ (e.g.\ $X\,\phi'$) there is no valid single-step projection,
and the translation is undefined.

\subsection{The \texttt{Composable} Class}

We abstract the specification algebra via a type class:

\begin{lstlisting}
class Composable a where
  concatenation :: a -> a -> a   -- (<>), unit: empty
  conjunction   :: a -> a -> a   -- (/\), unit: universe
  empty         :: a             -- identity for <>  (varepsilon)
  universe      :: a             -- identity for /\  (neg-emptyset)
  subtraction   :: a -> a -> a   -- (\\)
\end{lstlisting}

\noindent
The five operations form exactly the interface required by the bind
formula---no more, no less.
\lstinline{concatenation} sequences effects (used for \lstinline{post}
and \lstinline{pre}); \lstinline{conjunction} combines simultaneous
constraints (used for \lstinline{future}); \lstinline{subtraction}
computes the residual obligation (the Brzozowski quotient for the RE
instance).
\lstinline{empty} and \lstinline{universe} are the respective identity
elements and appear in \lstinline{pure}.
The class assumes \lstinline{(<>)} is associative with unit
\lstinline{empty}, and \lstinline{universe} is the unit for
\lstinline{(/\\)}; \lstinline{subtraction} satisfies
$\varepsilon \setminus r = r$ and $\univ \setminus r = \varepsilon$.

Normalization is \emph{not} part of the class.
It is an RE-specific simplification called internally by
\lstinline{subtraction}; a different \lstinline{Composable} instance
need not define it at all.

\subsection{The \texttt{Effectful} Type}

\begin{lstlisting}
data Effectful eff a = Effectful
  { ret    :: a
  , pre    :: eff   -- what must have occurred before this computation
  , post   :: eff   -- what this computation produces
  , future :: eff   -- what the rest of the program must do
  }
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Monad Instances}

\lstinline{pure} introduces a computation with no effect and no obligation:

\begin{lstlisting}
pure x = Effectful
  { ret = x, pre = universe, post = empty, future = universe }
\end{lstlisting}

The bind operator sequences two computations.
Let $e_2 = f(\ret(e))$.

\begin{lstlisting}
e >>= f = let fe = f (ret e) in Effectful
  { ret    = ret fe
  , pre    = pre e <> (pre fe \\ post e)
  , post   = post e <> post fe
  , future = (post fe \\ future e) /\ future fe
  }
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{Future condition.}
The formula

\begin{equation}
  \fut(e \mathbin{>\!\!>\!\!=} f)
  \;=\;
  \bigl(\post(e_2) \setminus \fut(e)\bigr)
  \;\wedge\;
  \fut(e_2)
  \label{eq:future-bind}
\end{equation}

removes from $e$'s future obligation those traces that $e_2$'s
postcondition satisfies, then intersects the residual with $e_2$'s
own future obligation.
When $\fut$ normalises to $\univ$, all obligations are discharged.

\paragraph{Precondition (Hoare-rule style).}

\begin{equation}
  \pre(e \mathbin{>\!\!>\!\!=} f)
  \;=\;
  \pre(e) \mathbin{<>} \bigl(\pre(e_2) \setminus \post(e)\bigr)
  \label{eq:pre-bind}
\end{equation}

The term $\pre(e_2) \setminus \post(e)$ is the \emph{residual
precondition}: the part of $e_2$'s precondition not discharged by
$e$'s postcondition.
When $\post(e)$ fully satisfies $\pre(e_2)$---i.e.\ the quotient
is $\varepsilon$---the composed precondition reduces to just
$\pre(e)$, mirroring the Hoare rule
$\{P\}\,C_1\,\{Q\}$, $Q\vdash P'$, $\{P'\}\,C_2\,\{R\}$
$\;\Rightarrow\; \{P\}\,C_1;\,C_2\,\{R\}$.
When $\post(e)$ does not satisfy $\pre(e_2)$, the residual is $\varnothing$
and the composed $\pre$ becomes $\varnothing$, flagging the violation.

As a special case, $\univ \setminus r = \varepsilon$ for any $r$: a
universal precondition ($\pre = \univ$) is trivially discharged.

\subsection{Intuition via an Example}

\begin{lstlisting}
malloc :: Int -> Effectful RE ()
malloc a = Effectful
  { ret = (), pre = universe
  , post   = Single (Atom "malloc" [Num a])
  , future = finally (Atom "free" [Num a]) }

free :: Int -> Effectful RE ()
free a = Effectful
  { ret = ()
  , pre    = Single (Atom "malloc" [Num a])  -- malloc(a) must precede
  , post   = Single (Atom "free" [Num a])
  , future = universe }
\end{lstlisting}

\noindent\textbf{Good program: \lstinline{malloc 1 >> free 1}.}
After \lstinline{malloc 1}, the state is:
\[
  \post = \mathtt{malloc}(1),\quad
  \fut  = \finally{\mathtt{free}(1)},\quad
  \pre  = \univ
\]
Binding \lstinline{free 1} (with $\pre = \mathtt{malloc}(1)$, $\post =
\mathtt{free}(1)$):

\textit{Precondition:}
$\pre(\mathtt{free}(1)) \setminus \post(\mathtt{malloc}(1))
= \mathtt{malloc}(1) \setminus \mathtt{malloc}(1) = \varepsilon$.
So $\pre = \univ \mathbin{<>} \varepsilon = \univ$.\ \checkmark

\textit{Future:}
$\mathtt{free}(1) \setminus \finally{\mathtt{free}(1)} = \univ$.
Intersect with $\fut(\mathtt{free}(1)) = \univ$: result $\univ$.\ \checkmark

\noindent\textbf{Bad program: \lstinline{free 1} alone.}
\lstinline{pure ()} has $\post = \varepsilon$.
$\mathtt{malloc}(1) \setminus \varepsilon = \varnothing$.
So $\pre = \univ \mathbin{<>} \varnothing = \varnothing$.\ \texttimes

\noindent\textbf{Bad program: \lstinline{malloc 1 >> malloc 2 >> free 1}.}
After binding \lstinline{free 1} to the composed
\lstinline{malloc 1 >> malloc 2},
the $\post = \mathtt{malloc}(1)\cdot\mathtt{malloc}(2)$.
The future of \lstinline{malloc 2} is $\finally{\mathtt{free}(2)}$,
so the residual future after \lstinline{free 1}
still contains $\finally{\mathtt{free}(2)}$, naming the leak precisely.

\section{The RE Instance}
\label{sec:re-instance}
%% =========================================================================

The \lstinline{Composable RE} instance maps library operations onto
\lstinline{RE} constructors and implements subtraction via derivatives:

\begin{lstlisting}
instance Composable RE where
  concatenation = Seq
  conjunction   = And
  empty         = Epsilon
  universe      = anything      -- = Not Bot

  subtraction Epsilon   r2 = r2
  subtraction (Not Bot) _  = Epsilon   -- Sigma* \\ r = eps (trivially satisfied)
  subtraction r1 r2 =
    let alph   = atoms r1 `union` atoms r2   -- combined alphabet
        evts   = firstWith alph r1
        step e = subtraction (normalize (derivative e r1))
                              (normalize (derivative e r2))
    in foldr Or Bot (map step evts)

normalize :: RE -> RE   -- RE-specific; not part of Composable
normalize r = {- ... algebraic simplification ... -}
\end{lstlisting}

The third clause handles the universal-precondition case:
$\univ \setminus r = \varepsilon$ for any $r$.
The general clause collects the alphabet from \emph{both} operands
before calling \lstinline{firstWith}: this ensures that an event
appearing only in $r_2$ (e.g.\ a future obligation) is still considered
as a potential first step when $r_1$ contains a complement.
Equation~\eqref{eq:first-not} then checks, for each candidate $e$,
whether $\lceil\partial_e(r_1)\rceil \neq \univ$---confirming that
$e$ can genuinely open a word outside $L(r_1)$---before the
derivative pair is explored.

\lstinline{normalize} sits outside the instance and applies the
following RE-specific laws, exploiting the complement constructor:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
Law & Applied to \\
\midrule
$\varepsilon \cdot r = r$, $r \cdot \varepsilon = r$ & \lstinline{Seq} \\
$\varnothing \vee r = r$, $r \vee \univ = \univ$ & \lstinline{Or} \\
$\varnothing \wedge r = \varnothing$, $r \wedge \univ = r$ & \lstinline{And} \\
$\neg\neg r = r$ & \lstinline{Not} \\
$\neg(r_1\vee r_2) = \neg r_1\wedge\neg r_2$ & \lstinline{Not} \\
$\neg(r_1\wedge r_2) = \neg r_1\vee\neg r_2$ & \lstinline{Not} \\
$\neg\varnothing = \univ$, $\neg\univ = \varnothing$ & \lstinline{Not} \\
$\varnothing^* = \varepsilon$, $\varepsilon^* = \varepsilon$ & \lstinline{Star} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\section{Examples}
\label{sec:examples}
%% =========================================================================

We demonstrate FutureCond across three domains.
Each example defines primitive operations annotated with
\lstinline{pre}, \lstinline{post}, and \lstinline{future},
composes them in the \lstinline{Effectful} monad, and inspects the
normalised annotations of the result.
A \lstinline{future} of $\univ$ and a \lstinline{pre} of $\univ$
together certify the computation is temporally correct.

\subsection{Memory Management}

\begin{lstlisting}
malloc :: Int -> Effectful RE ()
malloc a = Effectful
  { ret = (), pre = universe
  , post   = Single (Atom "malloc" [Num a])
  , future = finally (Atom "free" [Num a]) }

free :: Int -> Effectful RE ()
free a = Effectful
  { ret = ()
  , pre    = Single (Atom "malloc" [Num a])
  , post   = Single (Atom "free" [Num a])
  , future = universe }
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}
-- Good: pre = universe, future = universe (all obligations met)
simpleFree = do { malloc 1; free 1 }

-- Bad future: free(2) obligation remains
leak = do { malloc 1; free 1; malloc 2 }

-- Bad pre: malloc(1) precondition not met
freeWithoutMalloc = free 1
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Mutex / Lock Lifecycle}

\begin{lstlisting}
release :: Int -> Effectful RE ()
release m = Effectful
  { ret = ()
  , pre    = Single (Atom "acquire" [Num m])
  , post   = Single (Atom "release" [Num m])
  , future = universe }

-- Bad future: lock 1 not released
lockLeak = do { acquire 1; acquire 2; release 2 }

-- Bad pre: release without acquire
releaseWithoutAcquire = release 1
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Database Transactions}

\lstinline{beginTx} carries a \emph{disjunctive} future condition:

\begin{lstlisting}
beginTx = Effectful
  { ret = (), pre = universe
  , post   = Single (Atom "beginTx" [])
  , future = Or (finally (Atom "commit" []))
                (finally (Atom "rollback" [])) }
\end{lstlisting}

A program ending with neither \lstinline{commit} nor \lstinline{rollback}
retains the full disjunction as its residual future, naming both
outstanding alternatives.

\paragraph{Summary.}
Table~\ref{tab:examples} summarises the three domains.
Each bad instance surfaces as a non-trivial residual annotation:
a leaked resource yields a non-$\univ$ \lstinline{future},
and a missing precondition collapses \lstinline{pre} to $\varnothing$.

\begin{table}[h]
\caption{Example programs, bad instances, and residual annotations.}
\label{tab:examples}
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\toprule

\midrule

Memory      & \lstinline{free} without malloc   & $\univ$ & $\varnothing$ \\

\section{Monad Laws}
\label{sec:monad-laws}
%% =========================================================================

We verify the monad laws for the $(\ret, \post, \fut)$ triple under the
assumption that the \lstinline{Composable} instance forms a monoid for
concatenation (\lstinline{empty} is the unit, \lstinline{<>} is
associative) and that \lstinline{universe} is the unit for
\lstinline{/\\}.
The \lstinline{pre} field satisfies the Hoare-rule property
(Equation~\ref{eq:pre-bind}) rather than the strict monad-law equality;
we discuss this below.

\paragraph{Left identity} \lstinline{pure x >>= f = f x}.

$\ret$: $\ret(f\,x)$. \checkmark \quad
$\post$: $\varepsilon \mathbin{<>} \post(f\,x) = \post(f\,x)$. \checkmark

\begin{align*}
  \fut(\mathtt{pure}\;x \mathbin{>\!\!>\!\!=} f)
    &= \bigl(\post(f\,x) \setminus \mathit{universe}\bigr)
       \wedge \fut(f\,x)
     = \varepsilon \wedge \fut(f\,x)
     = \fut(f\,x). \;\checkmark
\end{align*}

The subtraction $r \setminus \univ = \varepsilon$ follows because
$\univ = \neg\varnothing$ and the special case
$\univ \setminus r = \varepsilon$ (when $r_1 = \univ$, the library
returns $\varepsilon$ immediately).

\paragraph{Right identity} \lstinline{e >>= pure = e}.

$\ret$: $\ret(e)$. \checkmark \quad
$\post$: $\post(e) \mathbin{<>} \varepsilon = \post(e)$. \checkmark

\begin{align*}
  \fut(e \mathbin{>\!\!>\!\!=} \mathtt{pure})
    &= (\varepsilon \setminus \fut(e)) \wedge \mathit{universe}
     = \fut(e) \wedge \univ
     = \fut(e). \;\checkmark
\end{align*}

\paragraph{Associativity} follows from associativity of \lstinline{<>}
and the distributivity of \lstinline{\\} over \lstinline{/\\}.

\paragraph{Precondition.}
The \lstinline{pre} field satisfies the Hoare-rule property: when
$\post(e) \vdash \pre(e_2)$ (i.e.\ the quotient
$\pre(e_2)\setminus\post(e) = \varepsilon$), equation~\eqref{eq:pre-bind}
gives $\pre(e \mathbin{>\!\!>\!\!=} f) = \pre(e)$, matching the classical
Hoare rule.
In general, the residual $\pre(e_2)\setminus\post(e)$ records precisely
which precondition obligations of the continuation remain unmet.

\section{Related Work}

\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Future conditions.}
Song et al.\ propose future conditions as a first-class extension to
pre/post-condition contracts~\cite{song2025futurecond}, formalising the
notion that a function may leave temporal obligations for its caller to
discharge. FutureCond realises this idea as a Haskell library, providing
a monadic interface and derivative-based propagation that the formal
system does not address.

\paragraph{Temporal property verification and repair.}
ProveNFix~\cite{song2024provenfix} uses temporal properties expressed as
regular expressions to guide automated program repair, verifying that
patches satisfy the specification. FutureCond is complementary: rather
than repairing programs post-hoc, it carries temporal obligations as
first-class values so that violations are surfaced statically.

\paragraph{Effect systems and monads.}
The \lstinline{Effectful} monad is related to effect handlers~\cite{pretnar2015}
and graded/indexed monads~\cite{orchard2014,katsumata2014} that track
effects in types. Our contribution is orthogonal: we focus on the
\emph{temporal} dimension of effects---what must happen \emph{after}---rather
than on tracking which effects are present.

\paragraph{Session types.}
Session types~\cite{honda1993,gay2010} enforce communication protocols
at the type level, including ordering constraints. FutureCond is more
lightweight: it operates at the value level, uses regular expressions
rather than types, and can be applied to any domain beyond communication.

\paragraph{Runtime monitoring.}
Runtime verification~\cite{leucker2009} checks temporal properties of
execution traces against regular-language specifications.
FutureCond is a \emph{static} approach: future conditions
are computed before any execution takes place, and the embedding of
LTL\textsubscript{f} into RE (\S\ref{sec:ltl}) avoids automata
entirely.

\paragraph{Typestate.}
Typestate systems~\cite{strom1986,bierhoff2007} track resource lifecycle
in types, requiring type-system changes. FutureCond is a library-level
approach requiring no language extensions beyond standard Haskell type
classes.

\paragraph{Brzozowski derivatives.}
Derivatives of regular expressions were introduced by
Brzozowski~\cite{brzozowski1964} and have found wide application in
parsing~\cite{owens2009,might2011} and model checking. Equation
\eqref{eq:deriv-not} extends the classical definition to complement;
our subtraction operator is a novel application to future-condition
propagation in a monadic setting.

\section{Discussion and Future Work}
\label{sec:discussion}
%% =========================================================================

\paragraph{Decidability.}
Equality of normalised regular expressions is decidable, so the
fixed-point computation in \lstinline{subtraction} terminates whenever
the derivative sequence is finite---which holds for the regular
languages used in our examples. Extending to context-free or
$\omega$-regular specifications remains future work.

\paragraph{Precondition monad laws.}
The \lstinline{pre} field satisfies the Hoare-rule property
rather than strict monad-law equality (Section~\ref{sec:monad-laws}).
A natural refinement is to make \lstinline{pre} a separate, non-monadic
annotation computed by a static analysis pass over the \lstinline{Effectful}
term, separating the behavioural monad from the precondition contract checker.

\paragraph{Integration with effect handlers.}
Lifting FutureCond into an effect-handler framework
(\texttt{effectful} or \texttt{polysemy}) would allow temporal
specifications to be composed alongside algebraic effect rows, opening
the door to modular verification of larger programs.

\paragraph{Closed-world alphabet assumption.}
The \lstinline{firstWith} function handles complement soundly via
Equation~\eqref{eq:first-not}, using the union of both operands' atoms as the
working alphabet $\Sigma_0 = \mathit{atoms}(r_1)\cup\mathit{atoms}(r_2)$.
This is sound under the \emph{closed-world assumption}: every event that
could appear is mentioned somewhere in the two REs being subtracted.
An event entirely absent from both operands is never explored, so
a complement RE that accepts traces beginning with such an event
will under-approximate.
A fully open-world implementation would accept an explicit $\Sigma$
declaration, enabling complement over an unrestricted event domain
and removing the closed-world restriction.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%% =========================================================================

We have presented FutureCond, a Haskell library that extends effectful
computations with future conditions---extended regular expressions with
complement that specify temporal obligations on the remainder of a program.
Two design choices make the approach work.
First, complement is integrated into the RE language with the single
algebraic law $\partial_a(\neg r) = \neg(\partial_a r)$, making the
universal language $\univ = \neg\varnothing$ a natural identity for
discharged obligations and enabling globally assertions---all without
automaton construction.
Second, the bind operator propagates preconditions in the Hoare-rule style:
the residual $\pre(e_2)\setminus\post(e)$ records which precondition
obligations of the continuation are not yet discharged.
The resulting system is lightweight (a single module, no language
extensions), composable (a standard \lstinline{Monad} instance), and
doubly informative: a residual \lstinline{future} $\neq \univ$ names
unmet obligations, and a residual \lstinline{pre} $= \varnothing$ flags
a precondition violation.
